## Key stats from the marathon
On July 26, 2026 I ran 42.53 kilometers through San Francisco in 5 hours and 14 minutes. During the race, I also gained roughly 1400 feet in elevation (give or take, 140 floors). It was my first marathon, and it is the newest entry in five years of Garmin data going back to May 2021. Before I wrote anything about how the race felt, I did what I always do when I see a large pile of numbers: I look at them to see if they have anything interesting to say. Before I dig more into the analysis, I will briefly review some key stats from the marathon.

A few things to notice here. My body temperature climbed steadily from 18°C to 28°C over the five hours and the cadence variations get noticeably denser in the rear half of the race. This is not surprising. The marathon course had much more elevation gain than what I had prepared for. While I knew that I would gain 1000+ feet, I didn't really know how it would exactly feel. After several intense climbs till I reached mile 20, I was out of gas and had to take several walking breaks. I am still not sure if it was heat catching up due to pacing in the first 20 miles or pure fatigue. I attribute this to deviations from the plan that I intended to follow for the run. My longest run was 20 miles, that too with several breaks after mile 17. I believe 3-4 more weeks of training would have helped me finish stronger than I did.

Interestingly, my heart rate holds in a tight 165-180bpm band for almost the entire race despite several elevation surges. While common knowledge is to run in the 140-165 bpm range, I almost always push beyond 180 and can sustain it for few hours. This should not be possible. One explanation for this could be that my Garmin is imprecise in recording data. Nevertheless, I still think I did a decent job managing effort/heart-rate through pace rather than being spiking it during the climbs during the marathon. It is clear me to though that I really need to invest in building a strong aerobic base. Also this is high time that I measure my VO2 max and track changes in it as I train. That is one of the key bottom line metric I am going to optimize for.

The 3-minute gap between elapsed time (5:16:28) and moving time (5:12:01) is small and as expected. I almost did not come to a complete standstill in the entire race. This includes even water stations. The other surprising number is a 4:33/km best pace against a 7:23/km average, a huge spread that probably came when I was running downhill after I crossed the Golden Gate Bridge. 

Average cadence sits at a fairly low 152 spm with a 0.89m stride. I believe cadence should be a bit higher and still don't know if this stride length is ideal. I am not sure if I leaned too much into stride length rather than turnover, which tends to load the legs more on the later climbs. I was trying to follow a conservative pacing strategy. I also drank way too much water/electrolyte. At each of the 14 stations, I drank 2 glasses of water (which were filled to 40-50 percent). One was electrolyte and the other was water. I don't know if this is what I am supposed to do.

Overall, I feel like I just went with the flow in this race and more or less during the entire training session. I did not do a single strength session, almost no mobility stretching, and didn't know anything about the science part of how to run, when to drink water, how and what to eat during training and before race day. The next race, whenever I do that, will be way more scientific and data-driven than this was. I am trying to convince my rational mind that all of this could have been achieved if I had a better Garmin watch as opposed to my 6-year-old Instinct which captures very basic metrics.

# A retrospective tour

The race is really just one data point in a much longer story, so the rest of this post steps back and looks at the five years of running that led up to it. First, a map of every city I have logged a run in. Then a look at the specific training block that built up to the marathon, from scattered false starts to the block that I actually remained committed to. Finally, a broader look at how my pace, heart rate, and cadence have shifted across all five years of data. I still don't know enough about running to make too much sense out of it and draw any definitive conclusions. I will try to learn a bit more and then interpret these. 

## Where I have run

Every city logged since 2021 is shown here, sized by total distance run there. Palo Alto alone accounts for nearly a third of the total distance, 700 kilometers across 94 runs, more than the next two cities combined. It anchors a cluster in the Bay Area that also includes Fremont, Menlo Park, and Mountain View. I lived in Fremont, and Lake Elizabeth was my favourite running place. In Palo Alto, I would just run around the neighborhood and Stanford. Towards the end, I did a few runs in San Francisco and fell in love with the city. The next largest cluster sits roughly 1,500 miles away, around Dallas and Fort Worth (Irving, Fort Worth, Denton County). I mostly ran in my office campus, which has a beautiful 2-mile trail. I also ran at Sam Houston Trail Park in Irving, another beautiful place. Other than that, thanks to BNSF, I was traveling extensively across the US. I did runs in places like Kansas City, Springfield, Fresno, Stockton, Amarillo, among other places. I also did two runs in Mumbai that do not appear on the map at all.

## The build up

In the following chart, I show weekly mileage and long run distance during the marathon-specific training block, from August 2025 through race day. The chart tells a story. A first attempt at structured training in the middle of 2025 where I thought I would run Dallas or Austin Marathon. However, due to work, it fizzled out after a few scattered months. Then I moved to the Bay Area, and the year-end break came. It was only in 2026 that I could stick to a plan and climbed weekly totals in the single digits in February to a peak week of 54 kilometers in late June. Over that stretch, the long run grew from 9 kilometers to a high of 32 kilometers.

## Five years of data

This is just data, and I am unable to make inferences from it yet. This is monthly distance, pace, heart rate, and cadence across every run logged since 2021. One thing I notice though is that my pace on short runs, between 3 and 8 kilometers, went from about 5 minutes 46 seconds per kilometer in 2021 and 2022 to roughly 7 minutes 50 seconds per kilometer from 2023 onward. That is nearly two minutes per kilometer slower. Meanwhile, average heart rate barely moved, staying between 164 and 171 beats per minute in every year. I attempted to run slowly, hoping to build the aerobic base, and expected heart rate to drop so that I could eventually run fast. 

The volume trend is more familiar. Distance grew from 126 kilometers in 2021 to nearly 800 kilometers already in 2026, with a brief stretch in 2024 when nearly half my runs were on a treadmill (29 of 65), a habit that had mostly disappeared by this year (10 of 100). To me, running on a treadmill is a punishment. I don't see myself being able to prepare for a marathon if I just had to run on the treadmill.

None of this makes for a dramatic finish line story, but that is sort of the point. The marathon itself was not the interesting event. It was the predictable output of five years of runs that kept nudging the same inputs: more volume, more patience, and, it turns out, actually resting on the recovery days. The goal now is to be more scientific and data-driven about running. Rather than reflecting after 5 years, I will try to review things on a 1-2 month cycle and take feedback from data to course correct; and for this, I need a new watch.
