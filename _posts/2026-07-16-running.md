## Key stats from the marathon
On July 26, 2026 I ran 42.53 kilometers through San Francisco in 5 hours and 14 minutes. During the race, I also gained roughly 1400 feet in elevation (give or take, 140 floors). It was my first marathon, and it is the newest entry in five years of Garmin data going back to May 2021. Before I wrote anything about how the race felt, I did what I always do with a large pile of numbers. I look at them to see if they have anything interesting to say. A few things surprised me, including the fact that on average, my short recovery runs are slower than my long runs. Below is the route through the city, the elevation/pace/heart rate/cadence traces from the watch, and the full stats breakdown from the race itself.

A few things stand out looking at these together. Temperature climbs steadily from 18°C to 28°C over the five hours, and the red walk-break markers on the cadence trace get noticeably denser in the back half, which probably suggests heat catching up with pacing rather than pure fatigue. Heart rate holds in a tight 165-180bpm band for almost the entire race despite two distinct elevation surges, roughly 1:40-2:15 and 3:20-4:00, indicating effort was managed on pace rather than being allowed to spike with the hills. The 3-minute gap between elapsed time (5:16:28) and moving time (5:12:01) is small, so the aid-station stops were quick and didn't add much wasted time. The most surprising number is a 4:33/km best pace against a 7:23/km average, a huge spread that reflects a short downhill. Average cadence sits at a fairly low 152 spm with a 0.89m stride. This suggests that I leaned on stride length rather than turnover, which tends to load the legs more on the later climbs. Altogether I feel I followed a conservative pacing strategy that ceded time to heat and hills rather than to poor training.


# A retrospective tour

The race is really just one data point in a much longer story, so the rest of this post steps back and looks at the five years of running that led up to it. First, a map of every city I have logged a run in. Then a look at the specific training block that built up to the marathon, from scattered false starts to the block that actually stuck. Finally, a broader look at how my pace, heart rate, and cadence have shifted across all five years of data.

## Where I have run

Every city logged since 2021 is shown here, sized by total distance run there. Palo Alto alone accounts for nearly a third of the total distance, 700 kilometers across 94 runs, more than the next two cities combined. It anchors a cluster in the Bay Area that also includes Fremont, Menlo Park, and Mountain View. The next largest cluster sits roughly 1,500 miles away, around Dallas and Fort Worth (Irving, Fort Worth, Denton County), a leftover from a stretch of work travel. Two runs in Mumbai do not appear on the map at all.

## The build up

Weekly mileage and long run distance during the marathon specific training block, from February 2026 through race day. The chart tells an honest story. A first attempt at structured training in the middle of 2025 fizzled out after a few scattered months. Then came a gap of about three months. After that came the block that actually stuck, climbing from weekly totals in the single digits in February to a peak week of 54 kilometers in late June. Over that stretch the long run grew from 9 kilometers to a high of 32 kilometers.

## Five years of data

Monthly distance, pace, heart rate, and cadence across every run logged since 2021. The clearest trend here is not speed, it is restraint. I compared runs of the same length. My pace on short runs, between 3 and 8 kilometers, went from about 5 minutes 46 seconds per kilometer in 2021 and 2022 to roughly 7 minutes 50 seconds per kilometer from 2023 onward. That is nearly two minutes per kilometer slower. Meanwhile average heart rate barely moved, staying between 164 and 171 beats per minute in every year. It is my attempt to deliberatly run slowly so that I can eventually run fast. The proof shows up on the long runs. In 2026 they average 7 minutes 2 seconds per kilometer, comfortably quicker than the 7 minutes 43 second pace on my short runs, most of which are recovery jogs rather than genuine speed work. The volume trend is more familiar. Distance grew from 126 kilometers in 2021 to nearly 800 kilometers already in 2026, with a brief stretch in 2024 when nearly half my runs were on a treadmill (29 of 65), a habit that had mostly disappeared by this year (10 of 100).

None of this makes for a dramatic finish line story, but that is sort of the point. The marathon itself was not the interesting event. It was the predictable output of five years of runs that kept nudging the same inputs: more volume, more patience, and, it turns out, actually resting on the recovery days. The next question worth asking the data is whether that gap between short and long pace closes at all before the next one.
