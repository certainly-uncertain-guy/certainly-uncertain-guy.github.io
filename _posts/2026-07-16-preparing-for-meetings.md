This summer I was mentoring an intern while also leading a tight deployment schedule. I had a weekly check-in with her, but between the two, I ended up missing a few of them. Towards the end of the internship, I sat down for an exit interview and asked her four questions: what she did well, what she would do differently, what I did well as a mentor, and what I could have done better.

Her main piece of feedback was that she wished I had scheduled more frequent check-ins, two or three times a week. I agreed with her, but I also was not sure, in hindsight, how much more available I could actually have made myself given everything else going on.

That is what got me thinking that if I cannot increase meeting frequency, how can I make limited meetings more effective. A lot of 1:1 time goes into walking through updates that could just as easily happen asynchronously. Moreover, sometimes I am anyways switching context far too often to be as focused on details as much as I would want to. Furthermore, even if I absorb all the details, I may need time to reflect more. I do better work reacting to a result on my own time than in the middle of a meeting. So this is an experiment, not a fix I am confident in yet: I do not know if it gives you more of what you were actually asking for, but here is what I want to try.

Going forward, I would like the work-update process itself to be asynchronous. Each mentee runs their project along the lines described [here](/mentoring/2026/07/16/running-a-research-project/). You do not need to follow it to the letter, markdown is fine instead of LaTeX, but I do want a Heilmeier Catechism writeup and an experimental log with the key figures and the reasoning behind them, written as you generate results rather than after. Write it yourself, without AI tools, and go through a few passes (write, read, edit, rewrite) until you are convinced it cannot get any tighter. Answering the Heilmeier questions that carefully tends to surface more clarity than you would expect.
